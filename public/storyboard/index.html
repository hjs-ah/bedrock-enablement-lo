Storyboard: Talking Bedrock with customers

The storyboard is the design record behind the module: who it is for, what a learner can do afterward, what appears on each screen, and how each part is measured. It matches the build scene for scene.

Audience

Inside sales representatives and sales leaders who are new to generative AI and need to hold a credible first conversation about Amazon Bedrock with customers, from startup founders to C-suite executives.

Performance goal

After the module, a rep can open a conversation by finding a use case, place it in one of three adoption zones, frame a model-selection discussion with seven criteria, and stay honest and useful when capacity is limited.

Design approach

  • Action mapping: begin with the on-the-job behavior and keep only the content that supports it.
  • Scenario-based practice: three branching customer conversations with a strong, a partial, and a weak answer at each decision, and feedback that explains why.
  • Stable framework, swappable reference: product details change quickly, so the seven criteria stay put and facts live in one content file with a verify-before-quoting note.
  • Learner control with guardrails: a contents list and a progress bar show where the learner is and let them return to any step they have unlocked. Continue stays locked until a step's activities are done, so the assessment cannot be skipped.
  • Accessible by default: keyboard operable, focus kept in place after each choice, screen reader announcements and labeled tabs and progress, color never the only signal, large touch targets, works at phone width, respects reduced motion.

Welcome

Orientation, about 1 minute

Objective
Set expectations and show what the learner will be able to do.
On screen
Title, a one-sentence promise, an illustration of the central exchange (the customer asks where to start; the seller asks about their work), four performance objectives, a plain summary of how the module is scored, how to move around, and a note that Bedrock facts change and should be verified before quoting to customers.
Interaction
None. Learners can resume where they left off or start over.
Feedback
None.
Assessment
None. The scoring summary is here so learners know what counts before they make a single decision.
Why this way
Objectives are written as behaviors the learner can do on a call, not topics they will see. Saying up front how scoring works removes guesswork.

The real question

Warm-up, about 2 minutes

Objective
Feel the difference between pitching a product and finding a use case.
On screen
A CEO says: "Our investors keep asking about our AI plan. Where do we even start?" Three possible replies.
Interaction
Choose one reply. The choice locks and feedback appears.
Feedback
Each reply explains why it works or doesn't. The strong reply asks about a process or customer moment.
Assessment
Unscored. It sets up the rest of the module.
Why this way
The line mirrors what executives said under investor pressure. Starting with it makes the learner's first decision the central skill.

Three zones

Concept and practice, about 4 minutes

Objective
Place a customer's use case in back office, middle office, or customer-facing, and know the first question for each.
On screen
Tabs for each zone with who it covers, examples, what to watch for, and the first question to ask. Then three customer statements to sort.
Interaction
Browse the tabs, then sort three statements into zones.
Feedback
Immediate, with a one-line reason for each statement.
Assessment
Unscored practice. Reinforced in the knowledge check.
Why this way
New sellers don't need an AI taxonomy. They need a quick way to decide what to ask next. Three zones is small enough to remember on a call.

Choosing a model family

Framework and tool, about 5 minutes

Objective
Use seven criteria to frame a model-selection discussion without claiming to pick the model.
On screen
Seven expandable criteria, each with a plain-language meaning and a question to ask. Then a four-question planner that returns a talk track.
Interaction
Open criteria at will. Answer four questions about an imaginary customer to see a talk track.
Feedback
The planner output changes with the answers and always ends with: the model choice is a test result, not a sales claim.
Assessment
Unscored. The final line is tested in the knowledge check.
Why this way
Sellers frame the conversation and bring in a solutions architect for the comparison. The planner models that division of labor and avoids model names that would go stale.

Conversation 1: the technical founder

Branching scenario, about 3 minutes

Objective
Diagnose a technical buyer's workload before pitching, and route depth questions to a specialist.
On screen
A startup CTO asks why she should move to Bedrock. Two decisions, three replies each.
Interaction
Choose a reply at each decision. The second decision depends on the conversation, not on the first choice.
Feedback
Strong, partly right, or weak, with a reason, then a short takeaway.
Assessment
Up to 4 points.
Why this way
Feature tours lose technical buyers. The scenario rewards questions about cost, speed, quality, and security reviews.

Conversation 2: the executive under investor pressure

Branching scenario, about 3 minutes

Objective
Offer a small, measurable first step and an honest account of cost.
On screen
A COO owned by a private equity firm wants an AI plan without a science project. Two decisions.
Interaction
Same as conversation 1.
Feedback
Same structure. Weak answers include fear-based urgency and unsupported savings claims.
Assessment
Up to 4 points.
Why this way
This is the conversation the case is named for. The strong path defines success first, then proves it in a pilot.

Conversation 3: the capacity-constrained launch

Branching scenario, about 3 minutes

Objective
Stay credible when supply is limited: no promises, gather the load facts, use the escalation path, set a check-in date.
On screen
A VP of Engineering hit throughput limits and asks for a capacity guarantee four weeks before launch. Two decisions.
Interaction
Same as conversation 1.
Feedback
Weak answers promise or predict availability. Strong answers are honest, specific, and give a next date.
Assessment
Up to 4 points.
Why this way
Capacity was a real constraint in 2024 and 2025. Treating it as a conversation skill keeps the content useful and avoids internal allocation detail.

Knowledge check

Assessment, about 3 minutes

Objective
Confirm the learner can apply the core ideas.
On screen
Five multiple-choice questions on opening move, zones, selection criteria, capacity conversations, and who decides the model.
Interaction
Choose one answer per question. Answers lock with immediate feedback.
Feedback
A one-sentence explanation for every question.
Assessment
1 point each, 5 points. Together with the scenarios, 17 points. Pass mark 80%.
Why this way
Questions test decisions, not recall of product facts.

Results and job aid

Close, about 1 minute

Objective
Report the outcome and leave the learner with something to use on the next call.
On screen
Score, pass or retry, a points breakdown, and a one-page job aid: three first questions, seven criteria, a capacity checklist, and the closing rule.
Interaction
Retry the conversations and quiz if under the pass mark. Print the job aid.
Feedback
Pass or not-yet message.
Assessment
Reports score and pass or fail to the LMS (SCORM 1.2) when launched from one.
Why this way
The job aid is the part reps will actually reuse. It prints cleanly on one page.
