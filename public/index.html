The board wants an AI plan. Where do we start?

That was the question executives brought to my sellers in 2024 and 2025. Amazon Bedrock was new, my team was learning it as fast as the customers were, and the people asking had investors waiting on an answer. This is how I got about 50 inside sales reps ready for that conversation, and the work I built to do it.

Bedrock had been available for two months, and it wasn't in anyone's pipeline.

I was a district manager for Inside Sales (Startups) at AWS. Bedrock had been available for two months, and our reps had created no Bedrock opportunities. The baseline was zero a month.

Each rep was carrying 60 to 100 opportunities, and about half were inherited when they received their new book for the year. A new service had to earn a place in a full pipeline.

Numbers are my own account of the team, approximate and role-level. No customer names or internal documents are used on this page.

Three things made it hard.

Everyone was learning at once.

Individual contributors and leaders both had to get enabled fast, on a service that was changing quickly. There was no experienced group to learn from, so we learned together.

Executives were under pressure with no starting point.

C-suite customers were answering questions from their venture and private equity investors about AI. Many didn't know where to begin. Sellers needed to offer a starting point, not a feature list.

Capacity was limited.

Across the industry in 2024 and 2025, demand for accelerated computing ran ahead of supply. For customers, that showed up as quotas, throughput limits, and model availability that varied by region and timing. Sellers were under pressure to advance and close deals while unable to promise capacity, and the conversation still had to be honest and useful.

Capacity is described in public, industry-level terms.

How I designed it

  • Start from the job. I began with what a rep has to do on a call and worked back to the least content that supports it (action mapping).
  • Practice over presentation. Three branching conversations carry most of the learning. Each has a strong, a partial, and a weak answer, and feedback explains why.
  • Keep the framework steady and the reference current. Seven selection criteria stay put when models change. Product detail lives in one file that can be swapped.
  • Plan the measurement first. Reaction, learning, behavior, and results (the four Kirkpatrick levels) each have a measure in the storyboard.
  • One build, two outputs. A web version for this page and a SCORM package for an LMS, from the same files.

What I'd tell another enablement leader

We earned the right to teach this by learning it in the open. Leaders and reps worked from the same material at the same time, which made it safe to ask basic questions.

Two choices mattered most. We trained the conversation instead of the catalog, so sellers learned what to ask, not what to recite. And we let sellers write the content, so it sounded like the calls they were actually having.

If I did it again, I'd instrument the pipeline from the first week, so the story of what moved, and when, would be easier to tell.
